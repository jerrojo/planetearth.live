# Norway — Planet Lens Profile

**Composite Score: 60 / 100 — YELLOW**
**Last updated: 2026-04-20**

---

## Overview

Norway is the paradox country of the Global North. On the domestic side it is the closest thing the world has to a built-in, pre-transition economy: electricity generation is ~98% renewable, almost entirely from hydropower, with growing onshore wind; EVs account for roughly 95% of new passenger-car sales, the highest share on Earth; air quality is excellent; and the Yale Environmental Performance Index 2024 ranks Norway inside the global top five. Forests cover ~38% of the country and are a large net carbon sink, roughly -20 to -25 MtCO2e per year, and Norway finances tropical forest protection at a scale few countries match through NICFI and the Rainforest Foundation.

Then there is the other Norway. It is a top-20 global oil and gas producer, Europe's largest natural-gas supplier after the loss of Russian pipeline flows, and the beneficiary of a windfall that has swelled the Government Pension Fund Global to roughly $1.6 trillion — about $290,000 per Norwegian. The exported barrels emit roughly 450 MtCO2e per year when burned abroad, close to ten times the territorial inventory of ~46.5 MtCO2e. The 2025 APA licensing round opened new blocks in the North Sea and Barents Sea, directly contrary to the IEA's conclusion that no new oil and gas fields are compatible with a 1.5°C pathway. Climate Action Tracker rates Norway overall "Insufficient" for exactly this reason: domestic ambition is strong, but the export side of the ledger is not being managed down.

Nature is under stress in less-visible ways too. Wild Atlantic salmon returns collapsed to a record low in 2024, and 33 rivers were closed to fishing in 2025. Open-net salmon farming is a major culprit, through sea-lice loads and escapees. Arctic warming is proceeding at three to four times the global mean, reshaping cod, reindeer and polar bear ranges. And the 2024 decision to open parts of the Norwegian continental shelf to commercial deep-sea mineral extraction — paused in early 2026 after sustained pressure — remains a live controversy.

## Top 5 Gaps

**1. Domestic emissions trajectory (~111% gap vs 1.5°C pathway).** Under current policies Norway is projected at ~38 MtCO2e in 2030. A 1.5°C-compatible domestic pathway is closer to ~18 MtCO2e. Most of the remaining territorial emissions sit in transport (heavy vehicles and aviation), industrial process heat, and offshore oil and gas extraction itself, which is powered partly by gas turbines on platforms.

**2. Exported fossil emissions (~450 MtCO2e/yr, roughly 10× the domestic total).** Norway's climate targets exclude combustion emissions from exported oil and gas. The IEA's 2021 Net Zero roadmap was explicit: no new oil and gas fields approved for development from that year onward are compatible with 1.5°C. Norway has approved many since, including fields under the 2024 "Yggdrasil" cluster and the 2025 APA round. Closing this gap is a political question, not a technical one.

**3. Marine protected areas (4.6% vs 30%).** Norway governs one of the largest exclusive economic zones in Europe and a rich Arctic marine realm, yet formal marine protection is thin and concentrated around Svalbard. Industrial fisheries, salmon farming, and proposed seabed mining all compete for space inside the unprotected ~95%.

**4. Terrestrial protected areas (17.5% vs 30%).** The current network leans heavily on high-mountain and high-Arctic reserves. Productive coastal, boreal and old-growth forest are under-represented, and OECM reporting is still in early stages.

**5. Wild Atlantic salmon collapse.** Returns have fallen to a historic low and the IUCN reassessment places them closer to "vulnerable/endangered" in parts of the range. Sea-lice and escapes from open-net cage farms, combined with ocean warming and hydropower impacts on spawning rivers, are the identified drivers. Recovery would require either enclosed-containment aquaculture or substantial production cuts, both contested.

## Recent Actions (last 12 months)

**Positive:**

- **November 2025:** Updated 2035 NDC submitted pledging a 70–75% cut vs 1990 — among the most ambitious in the UNFCCC registry on paper.
- **February 2026:** Parliament paused commercial deep-sea mining licensing round pending further environmental assessment.
- **January 2026:** Government Pension Fund Global tightened climate expectations and excluded additional oil-sands producers.

**Negative:**

- **June 2025:** APA 2025 licensing round approved new production licences on the Norwegian Continental Shelf, including Barents Sea acreage.
- **August 2025:** 33 salmon rivers closed after record-low wild Atlantic salmon returns, with open-net farms implicated.

**Neutral:**

- **October 2025:** Supreme Court reaffirmed that Fosen wind farm violates Sámi reindeer herding rights under ICCPR Article 27; government instructed to negotiate mitigation. A decarbonization project running into indigenous rights has become a signature tension of the Norwegian transition.

## What a Green Pathway Would Look Like

If Norway closed its top gaps, it would:

- Stop issuing new exploration and production licences on the NCS and publish a managed-decline plan for existing fields, aligned with a 1.5°C carbon budget.
- Extend the Climate Change Act to cover exported combustion emissions, or at minimum publish an annual export-emissions account alongside territorial inventories.
- Triple marine protected area coverage by 2030, with genuine no-take zones in fjord and coastal spawning habitat, and formally recognize OECMs.
- Require closed-containment technology for new salmon aquaculture licences and phase out open-net cages over a defined timeline to give wild Atlantic salmon a recovery pathway.
- Fully implement Fosen-type indigenous consent processes across all renewable build-out in Sámi traditional lands, rather than treating consultation as retrospective mitigation.
- Reject any re-opening of deep-sea mining until there is credible evidence it can be done without destroying abyssal ecosystems that are, by definition, irreplaceable on policy timescales.

## Data Confidence

**High.** Emissions, energy, protected area and governance data are all from multiple independent primary sources (SSB, Miljødirektoratet, Ember, OWID, Protected Planet, CAT, UNFCCC). Biodiversity and agriculture pillars are slightly softer, with salmon trends and aquaculture impact relying on national-agency data that is still being fully integrated into IUCN and FAO global datasets.

## Key Tension

Norway powers itself almost entirely on renewables and leads the world on EVs, yet exports enough oil and gas to emit ten times its domestic footprint — its climate credibility rests on whether it is willing to manage the decline of the industry that made it rich.
