# Uruguay — Planet Lens Profile

**Composite Score: 55 / 100 — YELLOW**
**Last updated: 2026-04-20**

## Overview

Uruguay is a country with two environmental faces, and the gap between them is the story. On one face, it runs one of the cleanest electricity grids on Earth: roughly 94% of power comes from wind, hydro, solar, and biomass, and the transition was executed in under a decade (2008–2017) through a state-led auction model now studied internationally as a template. The country has zero coal in its electricity mix, no proven fossil reserves, and no active fossil extraction. A flat landscape, strong Atlantic wind resource, and the Rio Negro and Rio Uruguay hydropower corridors made the geography favourable — but it was policy, not luck, that closed the deal.

On the other face, Uruguay is a cattle and pulp economy. Twelve million cattle (four per person) graze ~60% of the territory, and enteric methane from that herd is why agriculture, at ~74%, dominates the emissions inventory — one of the highest agricultural shares in the world. Soy and eucalyptus have both expanded rapidly since 2000: soy for export to China, eucalyptus to feed two multi-billion-dollar pulp mills (UPM and Montes del Plata). Tree cover has more than tripled since 1990, but almost entirely as monoculture plantation, not native forest, and plantation expansion has collided with rural communities over groundwater and soil acidification. In 2023, a severe drought dropped Montevideo's main reservoir (Paso Severino) so low that tap water was blended with brackish Rio de la Plata water — a crisis that surfaced how agriculture, forestry, and climate change meet at the faucet.

Uruguay is not megadiverse, but it holds a globally significant Pampas grassland — one of the most converted ecosystems on Earth, with >80% lost continent-wide to soy and pasture. Its protected area system (SNAP) covers only ~1.4% terrestrial and ~1.0% marine, among the lowest ratios in Latin America and far below the 30×30 target it endorsed. Democratic and governance indicators, by contrast, are among the region's strongest: Uruguay was an early signatory and ratifier of the Escazú Agreement, and the Orsi government (Frente Amplio, March 2025) has signalled stronger ambition on climate and water.

## Top 5 Gaps

**1. Protected areas (95% terrestrial gap, 97% marine gap).** Uruguay's SNAP covers 18 sites totalling roughly 1.4% of land and 1.0% of its vast 142,000 km2 EEZ — the single largest Planet Lens shortfall. The network exists structurally but has been chronically underfunded, and most of the Pampas grassland has no formal protection at all. Closing this gap would require both new sites and formal recognition of privately conserved and community-conserved land, since most remaining habitat sits inside large ranches.

**2. Agricultural methane (~74% of inventory).** The clean-electricity win is almost entirely offset by livestock methane. Uruguay signed the Global Methane Pledge but has no enforceable national pathway to cut enteric methane 30% by 2030. Feed additives (3-NOP, seaweed), rotational grazing, and herd intensification could deliver large cuts without hurting export volumes, but require public investment the beef lobby has historically resisted.

**3. NDC target architecture.** Uruguay's NDC uses emissions intensity per unit of GDP rather than absolute caps. This framing has let GDP growth mask absolute emission increases in the agricultural sector. An absolute cap aligned with 1.5°C would require either herd stabilisation or rapid productivity gains per animal.

**4. Freshwater quality and Santa Lucía basin.** The 2023 Montevideo drinking-water crisis was a climate-and-agriculture event: drought plus fertiliser runoff plus cyanobacteria in the upstream basin. Stronger riparian buffers, nutrient caps on dairy, and irrigation governance would reduce the probability of repeat crises.

**5. Monoculture plantation pressure.** Eucalyptus and pine plantations have been the engine of Uruguay's forestry boom and of its pulp exports, but they lower water tables, acidify soils, and displace native grassland biodiversity. Policy currently treats plantation expansion as climate-positive on a carbon accounting basis; a fuller ecological accounting would weigh water and biodiversity costs.

## Recent Actions (last 12 months)

**Positive:**

- **March 2026:** Orsi government launches National Water Plan update — caps irrigation withdrawals in Santa Lucía basin, mandates riparian buffers.
- **November 2025:** First green hydrogen pilot plant breaks ground in Paysandú, anchor of the 2023 H2U roadmap.
- **September 2025:** SNAP expanded 9% with two new coastal reserves in Rocha.

**Neutral:**

- **January 2026:** Updated Long-Term Climate Strategy submitted to UNFCCC — retains 2050 net-zero policy but not statutory.

**Negative:**

- **June 2025:** Cabinet approves 30,000-hectare eucalyptus expansion in Tacuarembó over local water objections.
- **October 2025:** Rural land-tax break extended for beef exporters — critics say it entrenches the extensive-grazing methane footprint.

## What a Green Pathway Would Look Like

If Uruguay closed its top gaps, it would:

- Triple SNAP coverage by 2030 and formally recognise privately conserved grassland — the single highest-leverage Planet Lens move.
- Convert its methane pledge into a funded enteric-methane program (feed additives, herd productivity, rotational grazing) with a binding 2030 pathway.
- Shift its NDC from intensity-per-GDP to an absolute cap, removing the escape hatch that growth has provided.
- Finish the electricity story — close the last 6% fossil gap with storage and demand-response — and make green hydrogen and e-methanol exports the new industrial identity.
- Tighten plantation licensing to require groundwater impact assessments and native-forest buffer reciprocity.

## Data Confidence

**High.** Emissions, energy, protected area, and governance indicators are well-covered by Ember, OWID, Protected Planet, and Yale EPI. The main uncertainties are biodiversity (Pampas grassland species monitoring is thin) and the exact magnitude of implicit fossil subsidies.

## Key Tension

Uruguay runs one of the cleanest electricity grids on Earth yet exports beef and pulp that make agriculture three-quarters of its emissions — its climate story is decided not at the power plant but at the pasture.
