# Iceland — Planet Lens Profile

**Composite Score: 55 / 100 — YELLOW**
**Last updated: 2026-04-20**

## Overview

Iceland is the planetary outlier that everyone wants to learn from and almost no one can copy. It runs on an electricity grid that is effectively 100% renewable — ~70% hydro, ~30% geothermal — and heats nearly every home with piped hot water from volcanic aquifers. It hosts Climeworks' Mammoth facility, the largest direct air capture plant on Earth, which mineralizes captured CO2 into basalt through the Carbfix process. And yet Iceland's per-capita greenhouse gas emissions — around 32 tCO2 equivalent when heavy industry and drained peatlands are counted — are among the highest in Europe, higher than Germany's and roughly triple the EU average. The gap between the clean grid and the dirty ledger is the story of Iceland's Planet Lens profile.

Three things explain it. First, aluminum. Iceland attracted three large smelters by offering long-term low-cost renewable power; the smelters are clean by the standards of the global aluminum industry but massive by Icelandic scale, accounting for roughly a third of national emissions directly and more indirectly. Second, peatlands. A millennium of drainage for pastures has left drained organic soils leaking around 8 MtCO2 per year — more than all the country's road transport and fisheries combined. Third, the fishing fleet and heavy transport, which still burn diesel and marine fuel oil because there is no grid for ships.

Iceland is also, ecologically, a reshaped country. At settlement in 874 CE it was estimated to be 25–40% forested; by the early twentieth century native forest cover was under 1%, eaten by sheep and cut for firewood. Active afforestation has pushed cover back toward 2% — real progress but still the lowest in Europe, and mostly non-native Sitka spruce and lodgepole pine. Globally, Iceland matters most for its seabirds: around 60% of the world's Atlantic puffins breed on its cliffs, alongside vast colonies of fulmars, razorbills, and Arctic terns.

## Top 5 Gaps

**1. 2030 NDC gap (44%).** Under current policies Iceland is projected to emit ~11.5 MtCO2e in 2030, versus an EU-joint target of roughly 8.0 MtCO2e (55% below 1990). Transport and heavy industry are the laggards — aluminum smelters are not covered by national reduction pathways in the same way other sectors are, and EV adoption, while high, is not yet fast enough to offset growing VKT.

**2. Drained peatlands (~8 MtCO2e/yr).** The single largest lever, and the cheapest. Roughly 4,200 km² of peatland has been drained historically; rewetting even half of it would make a disproportionate dent in the national ledger. The 2025 Climate Action Plan raised the target to 20,000 ha by 2030 — still only a small fraction of what is drained.

**3. Marine protected areas (0.9% vs 30%).** Iceland's EEZ is huge and ecologically critical, but strict marine protection is effectively absent outside seasonal fisheries closures. Converting some closures into permanent no-take reserves, especially on seamounts and cold-water coral beds, is the fastest path to catching up with the GBF.

**4. Native forest cover (<2% vs historical ~25–40%).** Not a carbon question alone — it is an ecological baseline question. Restoration is happening but slowly, and the tension between afforestation (for carbon) and native ecosystem restoration (for biodiversity) has not been resolved in planting policy.

**5. Commercial whaling.** Iceland is one of three countries (with Norway and Japan) that still hunts whales commercially. The 2024 fin whale quota of 128 was the lowest ever and only Hvalur hf. remains active, but the practice continues under short-term annual licenses in apparent defiance of the spirit of the IWC moratorium.

## Recent Actions (last 12 months)

**Positive:**

- **May 2024:** Climeworks Mammoth DAC facility inaugurated — 36,000 tCO2/yr, world's largest.
- **March 2025:** Parliament approved updated Climate Action Plan with expanded peatland rewetting target.
- **September 2025:** Landsvirkjun signed first commercial green hydrogen offtake deal with a European shipping consortium.

**Negative / neutral:**

- **June 2024:** Fin whale license renewed despite majority public opposition.
- **November 2025:** Capelin quota set to zero for third year — climate-driven stock collapse.
- **February 2026:** Environment Agency confirmed Iceland is off-track on its 2030 target.

## What a Green Pathway Would Look Like

If Iceland closed its top gaps, it would:

- Put the 2040 net-zero target into statute, not just policy, and align the Climate Act with that date.
- Launch a national peatland-rewetting program at scale — 50,000+ ha by 2030 — treating drained organic soils as a primary climate lever, not a side project.
- Announce a phase-out date for commercial whaling rather than renewing annual licenses, ending the diplomatic friction and aligning with the IWC.
- Convert a meaningful fraction of EEZ fisheries closures into strict marine protected areas, starting with seamounts, cold-water coral, and puffin foraging hotspots.
- Decarbonize aluminum in dialogue with the smelter operators — either via green hydrogen for alumina refining upstream or via inert-anode smelting technology, paired with honest accounting of the sector's share of national emissions.
- Scale the DAC + Carbfix mineralization model with transparent accounting, avoiding the trap of letting removals substitute for reductions in industry and transport.

## Data Confidence

**High.** Iceland publishes excellent, well-audited environmental data through Umhverfisstofnun and Hafró, and the country is covered in depth by Yale EPI, Ember, and Protected Planet. The softest areas are biodiversity trend data (small country, thin species monitoring budget) and fisheries climate-impact attribution.

## Key Tension

A country with ~100% renewable electricity and the world's largest DAC facility still has among Europe's highest per-capita emissions — because aluminum smelters, drained peatlands, and fishing fleets account for more carbon than the clean grid can offset.
