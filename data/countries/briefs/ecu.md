## Overview

Ecuador is a country the size of Nevada that concentrates a disproportionate share of the planet's living systems. It straddles the Equator across four ecological worlds — Pacific coast, Andean highlands, Amazon basin, and the Galápagos archipelago — and by most measures holds more species per square kilometer than any other nation on Earth. A single hectare of Yasuní National Park contains more tree species than exist in the United States and Canada combined, and the Galápagos remain the living classroom where evolutionary biology was first articulated. Ecuador is also a global legal pioneer: the 2008 Constitution was the first in the world to grant nature itself ("Pacha Mama") enforceable rights, and in August 2023 its citizens became the first electorate anywhere to vote — via binding referendum — to leave commercially viable oil reserves permanently underground, inside Yasuní's Ishpingo-Tambococha-Tiputini (ITT) field.

And yet, as of 2026, Petroecuador is still pumping crude out of Block 43-ITT. The Noboa administration, inaugurated into a country convulsed by a violence crisis and a collapsing hydroelectric grid, has missed the one-year constitutional deadline to wind down operations and is instead auctioning new Amazon oil blocks. A 2023–2024 drought exposed how fragile Ecuador's otherwise-clean ~72% renewable electricity mix actually is: reservoirs ran dry, diesel generators came back online, and rolling blackouts stretched to 14 hours a day. The promise of a post-extractivist future — once embodied by the Yasuní-ITT Initiative — is in retreat. Ecuador has written the most ambitious environmental law on paper of any country in the Global South, and is struggling to implement almost any of it.

## Top 5 Gaps

**1. Yasuní-ITT withdrawal (100% gap).** The most symbolically important environmental vote in democratic history is being defied by the executive branch. The Constitutional Court reaffirmed in February 2026 that the referendum is binding and ordered a dismantling timetable within 90 days. The outcome of that standoff — whether a direct citizen mandate can override a government hungry for oil revenue — is the single most important climate-governance test in the Americas right now.

**2. Primary Amazon forest loss.** Ecuador's Amazon is still ~80% intact, which is extraordinary, but the frontier is advancing fast. The drivers are a trio: the oil road network (roads bring colonists, colonists clear forest), illegal gold mining in Napo and Esmeraldas (dredges now visible from satellite), and cattle/palm expansion. Socio Bosque — the country's flagship payments-for-conservation program, covering ~1.6M ha — has been starved of budget since the oil-price collapse of 2015 and urgently needs refinancing.

**3. NDC ambition.** Ecuador's 2019 NDC targets a 9% unconditional emissions reduction by 2025 versus business-as-usual. It has no updated 2035 target, and what exists is not Paris-aligned. Submitting an absolute, economy-wide 2035 target before COP30 would close a credibility gap that allows the state to claim climate leadership internationally while expanding the oil frontier domestically.

**4. Terrestrial 30×30 (30% gap).** At ~21% coverage through the SNAP, Ecuador is closer to the Global Biodiversity Framework target than most tropical countries, but the last 9 percentage points are the hardest. Formal recognition of Indigenous and Afro-Ecuadorian conserved territories as OECMs would close much of this gap without expropriating land — but requires the state to share sovereignty.

**5. Fossil fuel subsidies (~$3.2B/yr, ~2.7% of GDP).** Ecuador spends more on gasoline and diesel subsidies than on its environment ministry several times over. Every attempt to phase them out — 2019, 2022, 2025 — has triggered a national Indigenous-led paro (strike). The subsidies are regressive but politically combustible; their reform is inseparable from a credible social protection floor.

## Recent Actions (last 12 months)

**Positive:**

- **February 2026:** Constitutional Court reaffirmed Yasuní-ITT referendum as binding and demanded a dismantling timetable from the executive within 90 days.
- **August 2025:** $150M debt-for-nature swap completed, channeling savings into Galápagos and Hermandad marine enforcement — the largest marine swap to date.
- **December 2025:** First Green Climate Fund REDD+ results-based payment tranche (~$18M) received.

**Negative:**

- **November 2025:** Noboa government opened a new oil licensing round adjacent to Yasuní and Cuyabeno.
- **January 2026:** At least 4 Indigenous environmental defenders killed in 12 months across Napo and Sucumbíos (Global Witness + local monitors).

**Neutral:**

- **September 2025:** Diesel subsidy reform partially reversed after nationwide Indigenous-led paro; fiscal impact on green transition unclear.

## What a Green Pathway Would Look Like

If Ecuador closed its top gaps, it would:

- Honor the Yasuní referendum — published dismantling timeline, wells capped on schedule, workers transitioned — making it the first country in history to shut down a producing oil field by democratic mandate.
- Submit an updated 2035 NDC with an absolute emissions ceiling and a no-new-oil-frontier commitment ahead of COP30.
- Refinance Socio Bosque at 3–4x current budget using debt-for-nature mechanisms similar to the Galápagos model, and expand coverage to all enrolled Indigenous territories.
- Formally recognize Indigenous and Afro-Ecuadorian conserved territories as OECMs to close the terrestrial 30×30 gap without land expropriation.
- Phase out fossil fuel subsidies in lockstep with a cash transfer floor, negotiated with CONAIE and urban sectors — not imposed unilaterally.
- Deploy state security into the Amazon to halt illegal gold mining (a driver of mercury poisoning and defender killings) rather than only into coastal cities.

## Data Confidence

**Medium.** Protected areas, forest loss, and biodiversity indicators are high-confidence (Protected Planet, Global Forest Watch, IUCN). Emissions and sectoral breakdowns draw on INEC and OWID but 2024 figures are interpolated. The defenders figure (4 killed) is likely an undercount given how reporting collapses in conflict zones; the real number is probably higher.

## Key Tension

Ecuador is the first country where citizens voted to leave oil in the ground and the first to give nature constitutional rights — yet a cash-strapped state facing a violence crisis keeps drilling, logging and mining the Amazon anyway.
