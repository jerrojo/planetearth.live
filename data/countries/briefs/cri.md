## Overview

Costa Rica is the world's clearest demonstration that a small, middle-income country can decouple development from environmental destruction. It is not on the formal megadiverse-17 list — that roster rewards raw territorial size — but it holds roughly 6% of known global biodiversity on 0.03% of the planet's land area, the highest density on Earth. It runs its electricity grid on ~98% renewables, with extended periods of 100% renewable generation; it has halved the rate of tropical deforestation and then reversed it, growing forest cover from ~26% in 1983 to ~57% today; and it pioneered the national-scale Payments for Ecosystem Services (PES) scheme that has since been copied across Latin America, Africa, and Asia. Per-capita emissions sit near 2.3 tCO2e — roughly one-third of the OECD average — and Climate Action Tracker rates the country "Almost sufficient," one of only a handful of nations anywhere that approach Paris-compatible ambition.

None of this is accidental. It rests on three political decisions that compounded over generations: the 1948 abolition of the army, which freed public spending for education, health, and later environment; the 1996 Forest Law, which banned the clearing of primary forest and established the legal architecture for PES; and a constitutional jurisprudence — through the Sala IV — that treats the right to a healthy environment as enforceable. The country also co-authored the Escazú Agreement, the Americas' first binding treaty on environmental democracy.

The story is not finished, and parts of it are under stress. Transport emissions have become the largest single source of CO2 as the electricity grid has decarbonized — roughly 41% of the national total — and EV uptake is lagging the Decarbonization Plan trajectory. Pesticide intensity in the pineapple and banana sectors is among the highest in the OECD, contaminating lowland aquifers. And the Chaves administration (2022–2026) has cut MINAE's operating budget, attacked environmental journalists, and repeatedly pushed to reopen the Crucitas open-pit gold concession, which the Legislative Assembly rejected in February 2026.

## Top 5 Gaps

**1. Transport emissions and EV adoption (62% gap).** Decarbonizing the grid was the easy part; decarbonizing mobility is the hard one. Transport is now Costa Rica's largest emissions sector, and EV penetration at roughly 3% of the light fleet is well behind the 2030 benchmark the Decarbonization Plan set. The March 2026 update of the National Electric Transport Plan is a credible re-commitment, but it needs to translate into charging infrastructure in the rural cantons, not only in the Central Valley.

**2. Agricultural pesticide intensity (90% gap).** The country's environmental brand lives alongside one of the highest per-hectare pesticide application rates in the OECD, driven by pineapple and banana exports. Yale EPI 2024 flags agriculture as Costa Rica's single weakest policy area. The transition will require phasing down the most toxic active ingredients (the "dirty dozen" chlorpyrifos, paraquat, etc.), scaling up organic and regenerative certification, and resolving the trade-off between export dollars and the coastal reef damage downstream.

**3. Net-zero statutory anchoring.** The 2050 target lives in the National Decarbonization Plan — a presidential policy instrument, not a law. That has been enough so far because every recent administration has re-endorsed it. But the current political cycle shows the risks: when a single administration is hostile, a policy-level target can be eroded without repeal. Codification in law would give the target institutional resilience.

**4. Closing the terrestrial 30×30 gap (8% gap).** Costa Rica is within striking distance of the Global Biodiversity Framework terrestrial target — 27.6% vs 30%. The remaining gap is less about declaring new parks than about connecting them: biodiversity corridors between existing protected areas, where the new PES biodiversity-corridor modality is now a credible funding mechanism.

**5. Environmental defender protection and press freedom.** Global Witness records one defender killing in the most recent 12-month window, and RSF has flagged the ongoing lawsuits against environmental journalists. For a country that co-authored Escazú, both metrics need to be at zero.

## Recent Actions (last 12 months)

**Positive:**

- **February 2026:** Legislative Assembly rejected reopening open-pit gold mining at Crucitas — the 2010 moratorium holds.
- **March 2026:** National Electric Transport Plan updated with binding 2030 EV and charging targets.
- **September 2025:** PES program renewed with a new biodiversity-corridor modality, adding 60,000 ha.
- **July 2025:** Cocos Island / Bicentenary Marine Management Area management plan approved with full no-take zones on ~54,000 km².

**Negative:**

- **November 2025:** MINAE operating budget cut ~12%, reducing SINAC ranger headcount.
- **May 2025:** Senior officials sued environmental journalist Álvaro Murillo over mining coverage — chilling effect flagged internationally.

## What a Fully Green Pathway Would Look Like

Costa Rica already scores green on the composite. To stay green as the decade advances, it would:

- Codify the 2050 net-zero target in law, so it survives administration changes.
- Close the transport gap by accelerating EV tax incentives, rolling out charging infrastructure into rural cantons, and electrifying the inter-urban bus fleet.
- Phase out the highest-toxicity pesticide active ingredients on pineapple and banana, with binding per-hectare reduction targets and organic certification incentives.
- Scale PES into the final biodiversity-corridor gaps between protected areas and cross the 30% terrestrial threshold before 2028.
- Restore MINAE and SINAC operating budgets so park enforcement tracks on-paper coverage.
- Reaffirm Escazú implementation with specific protections for environmental journalists and defenders.

## Data Confidence

**High.** Emissions, electricity, forest cover, and protected-area data are all from multiple convergent primary sources (OWID, Ember, SINAC, Protected Planet, FAO). The lower-confidence pillars are pollution (pesticide exposure data is patchy across watersheds) and agriculture (OECD pesticide intensity figures rely on import-weight estimates).

## Key Tension

Costa Rica built the developing world's most admired environmental brand on forest and electricity wins, but its pesticide-heavy agricultural exports and a sitting administration pushing gold mining and budget cuts test whether the green identity is structural or generational.
