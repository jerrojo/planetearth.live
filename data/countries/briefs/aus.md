# Australia — Planet Lens Profile

**Composite Score: 49 / 100 — YELLOW**
**Last updated: 2026-04-20**

## Overview

Australia sits at one of the sharpest contradictions in the planetary transition. Inside its borders, the energy transition is genuinely underway: renewables have climbed to roughly 40% of electricity generation — up 17 percentage points in five years — and the Albanese Labor government's Climate Change Act 2022 has, for the first time, put legislated climate targets into Commonwealth law. In September 2025, Australia submitted a 2035 NDC of 62–70% below 2005 levels, roughly doubling the ambition of the 2030 target. Domestic emissions are down ~10% since 2015, and the Safeguard Mechanism reform of 2023 placed declining baselines on the 215 largest industrial emitters.

Outside its borders, the story is very different. Australia is the world's second-largest coal exporter and third-largest LNG exporter. The Scope 3 emissions embedded in those exports — roughly 1.2 GtCO2e per year — are approximately three times Australia's entire domestic footprint, and they do not count against the national ledger. The Gorgon LNG carbon capture project, once marketed as the world's largest industrial CCS, has captured less than half its promised volume since 2019 and was quietly redesignated as a learning experience. The May 2025 approval of the North West Shelf LNG extension to 2070 — waved through by a Labor environment minister — captured the tension: climate policy at home, fossil expansion abroad.

Australia is also megadiverse. Roughly 87% of its mammals, 45% of its birds, and 93% of its reptiles are endemic, and the continent holds evolutionary lineages that exist nowhere else. Yet Australia carries the worst mammal extinction record of any continent since 1500, and the Great Barrier Reef suffered its fifth mass bleaching event in 2024. Koalas in Queensland, NSW and ACT were listed Endangered under the EPBC Act in 2022. The promised Nature Positive reform of the EPBC Act — the most consequential environmental legislation overhaul in two decades — stalled in the Senate in late 2024 after mining and state government lobbying.

## Top 5 Gaps

**1. 2030 emissions trajectory vs 1.5°C pathway (49% gap).** Australia is projected to emit around 395 MtCO2e in 2030 under current policies. A 1.5°C-compatible domestic pathway for a wealthy OECD economy implies closer to 265 MtCO2e. The 43% target is within reach but well short of fair-share ambition.

**2. Scope 3 export emissions (~1.2 GtCO2e/yr).** Australia's coal and LNG exports carry embedded emissions roughly triple its domestic total. Every credible 1.5°C pathway (IEA, IPCC AR6) requires no new fossil field development and a managed decline of existing ones. Australia continues to approve new coal and gas projects, including the North West Shelf extension to 2070.

**3. Coal share of electricity (43% vs <10% needed).** AEMO's Integrated System Plan envisions a full coal exit by 2038, but the trajectory depends on storage build-out and transmission permitting, both of which are running behind schedule in NSW and Queensland. The Capacity Investment Scheme is starting to close the gap; tender round 5 in February 2026 was the largest in Australian history.

**4. Terrestrial protected areas (22.1% vs 30% GBF target).** Marine protection is a genuine success story — 45% coverage thanks to the vast Commonwealth marine reserves around Macquarie Island and the Australian Antarctic Territory. Land-based 30×30 lags, and Queensland's continuing land clearance for cattle pasture drags the national number backwards each year.

**5. Explicit fossil fuel subsidies (~A$14.5B / US$9.6B per year).** The Australia Institute's 2024 tracker puts federal and state fossil subsidies at A$14.5B — roughly 0.52% of GDP — led by the fuel tax credit scheme, which refunds diesel excise to miners and graziers. G20 members pledged in 2009 to phase out inefficient fossil subsidies; Australia's remain among the highest per capita in the OECD.

## Recent Actions (last 12 months)

**Positive:**

- **September 2025:** Submitted 2035 NDC of 62–70% below 2005 levels — roughly double the ambition of the 2030 target.
- **January 2024:** Victoria ended native forest logging, taking ~1.8M hectares of state forest out of production ahead of its original 2030 deadline.
- **February 2026:** Capacity Investment Scheme round 5 awarded 6.4 GW of renewables + storage — largest single tender in Australian history.

**Negative:**

- **May 2025:** North West Shelf LNG extension approved to 2070 — one of the country's largest emitting facilities cleared for a 50-year lifespan extension.
- **November 2024:** Nature Positive Plan (EPBC Act overhaul) stalled in the Senate after mining and state-government lobbying; the promised federal environment watchdog has been shelved.
- **July 2025:** Federal Court ruled in Pabai Pabai v Commonwealth that the government owed no duty of care to Torres Strait Islanders on climate; plaintiffs have appealed.

## What a Green Pathway Would Look Like

If Australia closed its top gaps, it would:

- Tighten the 2035 target to at least 75% below 2005 and align the domestic trajectory with fair-share 1.5°C pathways.
- Declare no new coal or gas projects and set a managed-decline trajectory for existing export volumes, in line with IEA Net Zero.
- Legislate the Nature Positive Plan and establish an independent federal EPA with genuine approvals authority, including a climate trigger.
- Phase out the fuel tax credit and other explicit fossil subsidies, redirecting savings into a just transition fund for Hunter Valley, Bowen Basin, and Pilbara communities.
- Accelerate coal retirements under AEMO's ISP by pre-2035, backed by committed storage and transmission build-out.
- Expand terrestrial protected areas to 30% through Indigenous Protected Area partnerships and strengthen the "quality" of existing reserves by phasing out grazing in conservation zones.
- Complete the Uluru Statement commitments, giving First Nations genuine decision-making power over Country.

## Data Confidence

**High.** Australia has some of the world's best publicly available environmental data: DCCEEW's National Greenhouse Accounts, AEMO electricity data, the 2021 State of the Environment report, and transparent EPBC referral data. Biodiversity and agriculture pillars rely on a smaller number of indicators but primary sources agree.

## Key Tension

Australia is decarbonising its own grid at pace while remaining the world's second-largest coal exporter and third-largest LNG exporter — the domestic transition is real, but the export emissions it ships abroad are roughly triple its entire domestic footprint and stay off the national ledger.
