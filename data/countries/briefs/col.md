# Colombia — Planet Lens Profile

**Composite Score: 50 / 100 — YELLOW**
**Last updated: 2026-04-20**

## Overview

Colombia is a country of superlatives that cut in opposite directions. It is the second-most biodiverse country on Earth per unit area — home to more bird and orchid species than anywhere else — and simultaneously the deadliest place in the world to be an environmental defender, every year since 2019. It was the first major fossil-fuel producer to halt new oil and gas exploration contracts (2023) and now co-hosts the Fossil Fuel Non-Proliferation Treaty initiative, yet Cerrejón, one of the largest open-pit coal mines on the planet, just extended its operating license to 2034. The electricity grid runs ~73% renewable thanks to legacy Andean hydropower, giving Colombia one of the cleanest power systems in the Americas, while deforestation in the northwestern Amazon spiked ~40% in 2024 as armed groups filled the vacuum left by the fraying FARC peace process. Climate Action Tracker rates the country "Almost sufficient" — the strongest rating of any fossil-exporting Latin American economy — but the distance between Bogotá's policy ambition and the frontier reality in Caquetá, Guaviare, and Chocó is where Colombia's Planet Lens story actually lives.

Geographically, Colombia compresses an extraordinary range of biomes into a single country: the northwestern Amazon basin, the three Andean cordilleras, the Chocó biogeographic region (among the wettest places on Earth), the Caribbean coast, the Pacific coast, and the Orinoco llanos. This ecological density is matched by cultural density — 115 Indigenous peoples and large Afro-Colombian territorial councils hold or claim significant portions of the country's forested frontier, and their safety is inseparable from the survival of those ecosystems.

## Top 5 Gaps

**1. Deforestation (100% gap to zero).** IDEAM confirmed in late 2025 that 2024 forest loss rebounded sharply to roughly 170,000 hectares after a two-year decline, reversing the hard-won 2022–2023 progress. The driver is not smallholder farmers but organized land-grabbing for cattle, coca, and illegal gold in the post-FARC arc — Guaviare, Caquetá, and southern Meta — where dissident armed groups now negotiate access with the state. LULUCF is the single largest sector in Colombia's inventory at ~33% of total emissions; every other climate commitment is downstream of bending this curve.

**2. Environmental defenders killed (79 in 2023 — #1 globally).** Global Witness has ranked Colombia the deadliest country on Earth for defenders every year since 2019. The victims are disproportionately Indigenous and Afro-Colombian leaders defending titled territories against mining, coca, and timber incursions. Colombia ratified the Escazú Agreement in 2022 — the first binding Latin American treaty with defender protections — but implementation of Article 9 on the ground remains thin, and the Constitutional Court in January 2026 had to order the government to accelerate its plan.

**3. 2030 emissions trajectory (45% gap).** Current-policy projections put Colombia at ~245 MtCO2e in 2030, against the NDC ceiling of 169 MtCO2e. Closing this gap requires cutting LULUCF emissions roughly in half (so: halting Amazon deforestation), plus transport and agriculture reform. Halting new oil and gas exploration is politically historic but does little for near-term inventory.

**4. Terrestrial protected area (24.9% vs 30%).** Marine coverage has already cleared 30×30 thanks to the Malpelo and Yuruparí expansions, an underappreciated Colombian achievement. Terrestrial coverage is 17% short, and much of existing on-paper protection in the Amazon is undermined by weak state presence — a park on a map is not a park on the ground if armed groups set the rules.

**5. Fossil subsidies (~$4.4B explicit; ~1.2% of GDP).** Gasoline and diesel price stabilization mechanisms remain a significant line item. The Petro government has reduced gasoline subsidies meaningfully since 2022, but the diesel and ACPM gap persists. IMF's implicit subsidy calculation, which includes undertaxed externalities, is considerably larger.

## Recent Actions (last 12 months)

**Positive:**

- **February 2026:** Colombia co-hosted the Fossil Fuel Non-Proliferation Treaty negotiating conference in Cartagena with Vanuatu and Tuvalu — 17 nations now endorsing.
- **January 2026:** Constitutional Court ordered the government to accelerate Escazú Article 9 implementation for defenders.
- **July 2025:** Ministry of Environment launched "Heart of the Amazon" Phase 3 — $60M GCF-backed program with Indigenous co-management in Guaviare and Caquetá.

**Negative:**

- **November 2025:** IDEAM confirmed 2024 deforestation rose ~40% to ~170,000 ha, reversing two-year decline.
- **September 2025:** Global Witness 2025 again named Colombia the deadliest country for defenders; 79 killings in 2023.
- **June 2025:** Cerrejón coal mine license extended to 2034 after ANLA review — contradicts the administration's fossil-phasedown narrative.

## What a Green Pathway Would Look Like

If Colombia closed its top gaps, it would:

- Bring Amazon deforestation back below 100,000 ha/yr and onto a trajectory to zero by 2030, with military-civilian environmental brigades in the Guaviare–Caquetá arc and tenure security for Indigenous and campesino reserves.
- Fully operationalize Escazú Article 9 with a funded defender-protection program, prosecution of intellectual authors (not just triggermen) of defender killings, and Indigenous guard co-financing.
- Tighten the 2030 NDC gap through a transport electrification push (Medellín/Bogotá metros; BRT fleets), silvopastoral conversion at scale, and methane control in the oil-and-gas wind-down.
- Convert the Cerrejón license extension into a just-transition pact for La Guajira — binding retirement date, renewables build-out, and Wayuu co-governance of water.
- Extend terrestrial protected areas to 30% with genuine ranger capacity, not paper parks, and expand OECMs to recognize Indigenous and Afro-Colombian territories that already function as de facto conservation.
- Convert the halt on new oil exploration into a time-bound production-phase-out schedule, making the Fossil Fuel Treaty leadership credible at home.

## Data Confidence

**High.** Colombia has unusually strong domestic environmental data infrastructure for a middle-income country — IDEAM's deforestation monitoring is near-real-time, the Humboldt Institute and SiB Colombia run world-class biodiversity inventories, and the Sabin Center, Global Witness, and Protected Planet all publish country-specific series. Gaps are in implementation tracking (defender cases solved, OECM recognition) more than in measurement.

## Key Tension

Colombia has the strongest climate rhetoric in Latin America and the weakest frontier enforcement on Earth — halting new oil exploration is historic, but protecting the Amazon and the defenders who guard it is where the policy meets the bullet.
