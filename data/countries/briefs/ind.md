# India — Planet Lens Profile

**Composite Score: 35 / 100 — RED**
**Last updated: 2026-04-20**

---

## Overview

India is the hinge country of the 21st-century climate story. With 1.44 billion people and a GDP of roughly $3.9 trillion, it is the world's most populous nation and third-largest emitter in absolute terms — yet its per-capita emissions of about 2.9 tCO2e are less than a third of the global average, and its cumulative historical share since 1850 is only ~3.4%. That asymmetry gives New Delhi a legitimate grievance in climate diplomacy and simultaneously exposes how fragile the planetary budget is: if India follows the coal-powered industrialization path taken by the rich world, the 1.5°C ceiling collapses. If it leapfrogs, the global transition becomes plausible.

The Modi government has placed a large bet on scaling non-fossil capacity. Installed solar has grown from roughly 10 GW in 2016 to more than 108 GW today, and the 500 GW non-fossil target for 2030, combined with the Green Hydrogen Mission and the PM Surya Ghar rooftop program, represents one of the most ambitious build-outs in the emerging world. But coal still generates around 70% of electricity, the NTPC pipeline includes tens of gigawatts of new coal, and the pace of renewable addition has slipped below what the 500 GW goal requires. CAT assesses India's overall package as "Highly insufficient."

Beyond climate, India ranks 176th of 180 on Yale's 2024 EPI — a reflection of air and water crises that are now measurable in lost life-years. Delhi's winter PM2.5 regularly exceeds 300 µg/m³, 60 times the WHO guideline, and the Ganga and Yamuna remain heavily polluted despite multi-billion-rupee clean-up programs. On biodiversity, India is genuinely one of the world's great storehouses: tiger numbers have climbed past 3,600, one-horned rhinos are at record levels, and Asiatic lions are expanding. Yet protected-area coverage (8.1% land, 0.3% sea) sits far below the 30x30 Kunming-Montreal targets, and grassland and wetland biomes remain structurally neglected.

## Top 5 Gaps

**1. Emissions trajectory vs 1.5°C pathway (57% gap).** Current policies put India on track for roughly 4.4 GtCO2e in 2030; a fair-share 1.5°C pathway would land near 2.8 GtCO2e. The intensity-based NDC is easy to meet while absolute emissions keep rising, which is precisely what is happening — up ~42% over the last decade. Closing this gap requires an absolute emissions cap post-2030 and a firm date for peaking coal generation.

**2. Coal share of electricity (70% vs <25% needed).** The renewable build-out is real, but so is coal expansion: Coal India's output targets keep climbing, new thermal plants are auctioned under the RDSS umbrella, and state-owned DISCOMs still take cheap coal power on long PPAs. The political economy — Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Odisha royalties; 500,000+ direct coal jobs — makes retirement harder than addition.

**3. PM2.5 exposure (~52 µg/m³ vs 5 µg/m³ WHO).** The crisis is not just Delhi. The entire Indo-Gangetic plain from Punjab to Bihar experiences the worst air on Earth for 4–5 months a year. The National Clean Air Programme set a 40% PM cut by 2026, but emission inventories are weak, stubble-burning enforcement is political dynamite, and coal captive power at industrial clusters remains largely uncontrolled.

**4. Marine and terrestrial protected areas (8.1% / 0.3% vs 30%).** India's EEZ is one of the world's most biodiverse and most intensively fished; marine PA coverage near zero is a glaring gap. On land, savannas, Banni grasslands, and high-altitude Himalayan ecosystems are chronically under-protected relative to forest reserves.

**5. Fossil fuel subsidies (~$39B explicit; much larger implicit).** IISD estimates explicit fossil subsidies, including LPG, fertilizer-urea, and oil-company under-recoveries, at roughly $39B in 2023 — roughly eight times clean-energy subsidies. IMF implicit subsidies, counting untaxed externalities, put India among the top five globally.

## Recent Actions (last 12 months)

**Positive:**

- **April 2025:** Carbon Credit Trading Scheme compliance-market rules notified for 9 energy-intensive sectors beginning FY2026.
- **July 2025:** Supreme Court expanded interim protection for unclassed forest land against the 2023 Forest Conservation Amendment.
- **September 2025:** Non-fossil installed capacity crossed 220 GW; rooftop solar enrollments under PM Surya Ghar doubled year-on-year.
- **February 2026:** Union Budget 2026-27 expanded solar rooftop and green-hydrogen allocations.

**Negative:**

- **June 2025:** Environment ministry cleared the Great Nicobar port-airport-township project over 130 sq km of rainforest despite tribal and ecological objections.
- **November 2025:** Delhi-NCR breached AQI 450 for 11 days; GRAP Stage IV invoked for the sixth consecutive winter, underscoring the lack of structural progress.

## What a Green Pathway Would Look Like

If India closed its top gaps, it would:

- Enshrine the 2070 net-zero target in statute and add an absolute GHG cap for 2035 that bends the curve rather than tracking intensity.
- Publish a coal-retirement schedule paired with just-transition financing for Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Odisha, and West Bengal, rather than the current "renewables added, coal preserved" posture.
- Rebuild fertilizer subsidies around soil-health outcomes (NBS-style balanced nutrients, natural farming) to cut N2O and revive the Indo-Gangetic aquifer.
- Treble terrestrial PA coverage through OECM recognition of sacred groves, community forests, and van-panchayats, and create a first-generation marine PA network in the EEZ.
- Restore the pre-2020 EIA framework — mandatory public hearings, no ex-post regularization — and lift FCRA constraints on environmental civil society.
- Fund the NCAP at 3–4x current levels and add enforceable airshed-level caps across the Indo-Gangetic plain.

## Data Confidence

**Medium.** Emissions, energy, and protected-area figures are high-confidence (multiple primary sources agree). Forest cover, biodiversity, and defender data carry documented methodological disputes: the Forest Survey of India's 21.7% headline is contested by Global Forest Watch; FCRA-constrained NGOs cannot monitor openly; and Global Witness numbers in India likely undercount given reporting barriers in conflict states.

## Key Tension

India must deliver development to 1.4 billion people on a fraction of the historical emissions of rich nations while its coal-heavy grid, filthy air, and drying rivers make its development model itself the climate risk.
