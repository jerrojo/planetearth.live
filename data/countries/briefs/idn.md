# Indonesia — Planet Lens Profile

**Composite Score: 32 / 100 — RED**
**Last updated: 2026-04-20**

---

## Overview

Indonesia is a planetary keystone that sits uncomfortably inside the global energy transition it is helping to supply. The archipelago stretches more than 5,000 kilometres across the equator, holds the world's third-largest tract of tropical rainforest after the Amazon and the Congo, and stores roughly 36% of all tropical peatland carbon on Earth. Its waters sit at the centre of the Coral Triangle, the single richest marine ecosystem on the planet. And its nickel, cobalt, and bauxite now underwrite the batteries and stainless steel that every climate plan outside its borders depends on.

That dual role — rainforest guardian and extractive supplier — is what makes the picture so difficult. Land-use change and forestry (LULUCF) still represent about 28% of national emissions in a typical year and can spike dramatically during El Niño fire seasons, as 2015 and 2019 demonstrated. Coal provides 61% of electricity, and a wave of "captive" coal plants — built inside special economic zones to power nickel smelters for EV supply chains — has effectively added a new fossil fleet outside the commitments made under the $20 billion Just Energy Transition Partnership. Primary forest loss fell to a two-decade low in 2024, a genuine success for the permanent moratorium, but 2025 satellite data from Global Forest Watch already show a rebound tied to mining, palm oil expansion, and the Nusantara capital project in East Kalimantan. Climate Action Tracker rates Indonesia's overall policy package "Critically insufficient" — the weakest tier in the G20.

## Top 5 Gaps

**1. Emissions trajectory vs 1.5°C pathway (93% gap).** Indonesia's projected 2030 emissions of ~1.45 GtCO2e dwarf the ~0.75 GtCO2e that a fair-share 1.5°C pathway would allow. Closing the gap is not primarily about the power sector — it is about halting forest and peat loss permanently, and refusing to let captive coal power the nickel boom. Neither is currently on track.

**2. Coal share of electricity (61% vs under 10% needed).** The JETP was meant to bend this curve, but implementation has been bogged down by disagreements over grants-versus-loans ratios, the perimeter of "captive" plants, and who bears the stranded-asset risk of early retirement for plants owned by state utility PLN. In March 2025, the government approved another 21 GW of captive coal capacity in Sulawesi and North Maluku industrial parks — a volume larger than the entire coal fleet of the United Kingdom.

**3. Marine protected areas (3.1% vs 30%).** Indonesia's 6 million km² of territorial and EEZ waters contain more coral species than anywhere else on Earth. The country's own 30×30 marine target is set for 2045, a full fifteen years after the Global Biodiversity Framework's date. Destructive fishing, sedimentation from coastal deforestation, and reef bleaching compound the protection deficit.

**4. Terrestrial protected areas (12.2% vs 30%).** A sizeable share of Indonesia's legally protected land is "paper" — gazetted but poorly enforced against encroachment by mining, palm, and plantation interests. Less than 5% of customary (adat) Indigenous territories are formally recognised despite Constitutional Court rulings affirming the right. The Indonesian 30×30 story is as much about tenure as about new parks.

**5. Fossil fuel subsidies (~$33B/year; ~2.3% of GDP).** Indonesia is one of the few major economies where fossil fuel subsidies are structurally larger than renewable energy investment. Subsidised diesel, gasoline, and LPG are politically hard-wired into the cost of living, and reform attempts (2014, 2022) have triggered protests. Redirecting even a fraction toward a just transition fund is the fiscal lever most likely to unlock faster decarbonisation.

## Recent Actions (last 12 months)

**Positive:**

- **February 2026:** Constitutional Court ordered formal recognition of 1.2 million hectares of adat forest, a landmark for Indigenous land rights.
- **June 2025:** Expanded mangrove rehabilitation programme under the FOLU Net Sink plan, targeting 600,000 ha by 2030.

**Negative:**

- **November 2025:** Prabowo government signalled it may exit or renegotiate JETP, citing grant/loan ratios and conditionality.
- **August 2025:** Nusantara capital inaugurated in East Kalimantan with a 256,000 ha concession overlapping peatland and orangutan habitat.
- **March 2025:** 21 GW of new captive coal approved in nickel-processing industrial parks outside JETP perimeter.
- **January 2026:** Ministry of Environment and Forestry merged under upstream energy oversight, weakening independent environmental review.

## What a Green Pathway Would Look Like

If Indonesia closed its top gaps, it would:

- Translate the 2060 net-zero pledge into binding legislation with sectoral carbon budgets for power, industry, and land use.
- Bring captive coal plants inside the JETP perimeter, cap new approvals, and publish a plant-by-plant retirement schedule for PLN coal before 2030.
- Make the forest moratorium enforceable on existing concessions — not just new ones — with public concession-level monitoring and penalties that exceed the value of illegal clearance.
- Accelerate marine 30×30 from 2045 to 2035, starting with the Coral Triangle heartland (Raja Ampat, Banda, Komodo) and genuine no-take zones.
- Formally recognise adat territories at scale (current ~4.5% of claimed customary land) as the most cost-effective forest protection measure available.
- Phase down the ~$33B/year fossil subsidy envelope gradually, with compensating cash transfers for the bottom two income deciles, and ring-fence savings for renewables and a coal-worker transition fund.

## Data Confidence

**Medium.** Emissions, energy mix, and protected-area coverage rely on convergent sources (OWID, Ember, Protected Planet, Global Forest Watch). Peatland and fire emissions have substantial year-on-year volatility that a single-year snapshot underrepresents. Defender and litigation counts almost certainly undercount — Global Witness consistently flags Indonesia as a high-risk country where reprisals are underreported, and Sabin Center's jurisdictional coverage is less complete for Southeast Asia than for OECD economies.

## Key Tension

Indonesia is being asked to protect the world's third-largest rainforest and third-largest tropical peat store while simultaneously becoming the electric-vehicle supply chain's nickel backbone — an export boom now powered by captive coal that sits outside its headline climate pledges.
