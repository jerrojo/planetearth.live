## Overview

The United Arab Emirates sits at the sharpest contradiction in global climate diplomacy. In December 2023, as COP28 host, it delivered the "UAE Consensus" — the first UNFCCC decision text in three decades of climate negotiations to explicitly call for "transitioning away from fossil fuels in energy systems." The same year, ADNOC — whose CEO Sultan Al Jaber presided over those negotiations — announced an acceleration of its oil production capacity to 5 million barrels per day by 2027, making it one of the most aggressive oil-sector expansions on the planet outside the United States. Both statements are true, and the Planet Lens registers both.

Per-capita emissions of ~24.5 tCO2e are among the highest on Earth — roughly 2.5× the Chinese figure, 4× the global average, and structurally driven by desalination (which supplies ~42% of drinking water), air-conditioning demand in one of the hottest inhabited climates on Earth, and a petrochemical-and-export economy. Absolute emissions have risen ~9% over the last five years and ~22% over the last decade. The Emirates is simultaneously home to Masdar, one of the world's largest renewable energy developers; to Al Dhafra Solar, among the lowest-cost utility-scale solar installations ever financed; and to the Barakah nuclear complex, whose four units now supply roughly a quarter of national electricity carbon-free. Yet renewables are only ~8% of the power mix. The clean assets exist; the fossil commitments keep pace.

Biologically, the UAE is not megadiverse, but it is ecologically distinctive. The Arabian oryx — extinct in the wild in 1972 — was reintroduced here. Dugongs graze the seagrass meadows off Abu Dhabi in the second-largest aggregation anywhere. Hawksbill turtles nest on Sir Bu Nair. Mangrove cover in Abu Dhabi has grown ~35% since 2010 through one of the most ambitious coastal-restoration programs in the Gulf, anchored by a pledge to plant 100 million mangroves by 2030. Against that, coral reefs in the southern Gulf endure chronic thermal stress — this is the hottest sea in which reef-building corals survive — and desalination brine discharge adds to the pressure.

## Top 5 Gaps

**1. Emissions vs 1.5°C pathway (64% gap).** Current policies project ~230 MtCO2e in 2030; a 1.5°C-compatible pathway requires ~140 MtCO2e. Closing the gap requires converting the NDC's BAU-referenced pledge into absolute terms and halting new oil and gas capacity approvals — both steps the Emirates has resisted.

**2. Renewables in electricity (8% vs >60% needed).** Masdar's international portfolio is larger than its domestic footprint: the UAE exports clean-energy expertise while its own grid remains three-quarters fossil. Al Dhafra (2 GW) and Noor Abu Dhabi (1.2 GW) are world-class installations, but more nuclear than solar powered the grid in 2025.

**3. ADNOC oil expansion.** The IEA's 2021 Net Zero by 2050 pathway is unambiguous: no new oil or gas field developments are compatible with 1.5°C. ADNOC's plan to expand capacity to 5 Mb/d — and to accelerate that target by a year — is the clearest signal of policy direction, and it points away from Paris.

**4. Marine protected areas (12% vs 30%).** Better than global average but still 60% short of the GBF target the UAE endorsed. The southern Gulf's ecosystems are under compounding pressure from heat, salinity, coastal reclamation, and shipping; genuine no-take zoning is limited.

**5. Fossil fuel subsidies (~$5B explicit; far larger implicit).** Domestic fuel and electricity pricing includes substantial producer and consumer subsidies; IMF's implicit-subsidy methodology (which prices in air-quality and climate externalities) places the UAE among the highest per-capita fossil subsidy jurisdictions on Earth.

## Recent Actions (last 12 months)

**Positive:**

- **November 2025:** Al Dhafra Solar PV (2 GW) fully commissioned at a record-low tariff.
- **December 2025:** Barakah Unit 4 enters commercial operation — completes 5.6 GW nuclear fleet supplying ~25% of UAE electricity.
- **September 2025:** Third NDC submitted with first-ever sector-specific sub-targets.
- **January 2026:** Federal Decree-Law No. 11 of 2024 on Climate Change enters force — first binding UAE climate law with penalties for non-compliance.

**Negative:**

- **February 2026:** ADNOC accelerates 5 Mb/d oil capacity target to 2027.
- **March 2026:** Ruwais LNG export terminal (9.6 Mtpa) approved — three-decade-life fossil infrastructure locked in.

## What a Green Pathway Would Look Like

If the UAE closed its top gaps, it would:

- Convert the 2035 NDC from a BAU-referenced pledge into an absolute-emissions target, and align it with a ~50% cut from 2019 levels.
- Freeze new upstream oil and gas approvals; redirect ADNOC's surplus free cash into domestic renewables, green hydrogen, and grid storage at Masdar-scale speed.
- Lift domestic renewables from 8% to >40% of electricity by 2030 through a national PV-plus-storage auction program modeled on Al Dhafra's economics.
- Extend marine protected areas from ~12% to 30% with binding no-take zones in critical seagrass and coral areas, and write mangrove restoration into coastal planning law.
- Phase out explicit fuel and electricity subsidies, using the savings to finance desalination efficiency and low-income cooling support.
- Open the climate policy space to independent science and civil society — including protections for whistleblowers inside state-owned enterprises — so that "transition away" can be audited, not merely declared.

## Data Confidence

**Medium.** Emissions, energy, and protected-area data are high-confidence. Biodiversity figures rely on limited Red List coverage and surveys skewed toward Abu Dhabi; pollution data is partially self-reported; defender statistics read zero, which reflects reporting constraints rather than the absence of risk for environmental voices inside the country.

## Key Tension

The UAE chaired COP28 and delivered the first UNFCCC text calling to "transition away from fossil fuels" — while its national oil company simultaneously accelerates the largest oil capacity expansion of any producer outside the United States.
