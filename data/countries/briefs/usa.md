# United States — Planet Lens Profile

**Composite Score: 42 / 100 — YELLOW**
**Last updated: 2026-04-20**

---

## Overview

The United States is the hinge state of the climate problem, and in 2026 that hinge is swinging the wrong way. It is the largest cumulative emitter in human history — roughly 24% of all CO2 released since 1850 — and still the second-largest annual emitter at ~5.9 GtCO2e. Its per-capita footprint of 17.5 tonnes is about twice China's and nearly three times the EU average. For a brief window between 2022 and late 2024, the US offered the closest thing the world had seen to an industrial-policy answer to decarbonization: the Inflation Reduction Act, the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law, and the CHIPS Act together committed more than half a trillion dollars to clean energy, transmission, EV charging, domestic battery and solar manufacturing, and methane abatement. Modelers at Rhodium and Princeton projected a plausible path toward the 50–52% NDC.

That trajectory broke on January 20, 2025. Within hours of the second inauguration, Executive Order 14162 initiated a second US withdrawal from the Paris Agreement and EO 14154 "Unleashing American Energy" paused IRA and BIL disbursements, revoked prior climate executive orders, and directed a sweeping expansion of federal oil and gas leasing. In March 2025, EPA Administrator Zeldin announced 31 major rule rollbacks in a single day, including the 2024 power plant greenhouse gas rule, vehicle standards, and updated mercury and air toxics limits. Climate Action Tracker downgraded the US from "Insufficient" to "Critically insufficient" — its worst category for a major economy. The underlying economic momentum of solar, wind, storage, and EVs has not evaporated (Texas is still leading the country in new solar additions), but the federal scaffolding around it is being removed mid-build.

## Top 5 Gaps

**1. 2030 emissions vs NDC (64% gap).** Under current policies, the US is on track for roughly 5.4 GtCO2e in 2030 versus the ~3.3 GtCO2e implied by a 50–52% cut from 2005. The IRA, if fully implemented, narrowed this gap to roughly 30–35%; the 2025 pause has reopened most of it. A credible closure now depends on whether IRA tax credits survive Congress, whether state-level action scales, and whether EPA rule rescissions are blocked in federal court.

**2. Per-capita emissions (661% gap from 1.5°C per-capita).** Even if the US hit its NDC, each American would still emit roughly 10 tCO2e per year in 2030 — more than four times the ~2.3 tCO2e per-capita global average that a 1.5°C pathway requires. This is the deepest structural gap in the US profile and touches housing, vehicle size, meat consumption, and flying, not just the electricity mix.

**3. Protected terrestrial area (57% gap from 30x30).** At roughly 13% of terrestrial area formally protected, the US trails most OECD peers. The 2025 administration has moved to shrink Bears Ears, Grand Staircase-Escalante, and the newer Chuckwalla monument, and has opened Arctic, offshore, and previously withdrawn federal lands to leasing. Marine protection at ~23% is better, but concentrated in remote Pacific monuments rather than distributed across US coastal ecosystems.

**4. Explicit fossil fuel subsidies (~$20B/yr).** The US has maintained expensing of intangible drilling costs, percentage depletion for independent producers, the foreign tax credit for oil and gas, and other preferences worth roughly $20B annually — unchanged for decades despite the 2009 G20 phase-out pledge.

**5. Implicit fossil fuel subsidies (~$760B/yr per IMF).** Once undertaxed externalities (health damage from combustion, climate damage, unpriced congestion) are counted, the IMF estimates US implicit fossil subsidies at roughly $760B annually — about 2.6% of GDP and the largest in absolute terms of any country.

## Recent Actions (last 12 months)

**Positive:**

- **September 2025:** The US Climate Alliance of 23 states plus DC submitted a subnational NDC pledging to meet the federal 50–52% target at the state level — a legal first.
- **June 2025:** US cumulative solar capacity crossed 250 GW; Texas leads new additions despite state-level hostility, reflecting the durability of underlying economics.
- **February 2026:** Supreme Court declined to block Hawaii's youth climate settlement, preserving the state's landmark decarbonization consent decree for transportation.

**Negative:**

- **January 2025:** Paris Agreement withdrawal initiated.
- **January 2025:** EO "Unleashing American Energy" paused IRA/BIL disbursements and expanded federal fossil leasing.
- **March 2025:** EPA announced 31 environmental rule rollbacks in a single day.
- **April 2025:** USDA cancelled the $3B Partnerships for Climate-Smart Commodities program.

## What a Green Pathway Would Look Like

If the US closed its top gaps, it would:

- Protect IRA tax credits in full through 2035 and reinstate the 2024 power plant rule, getting 2030 emissions back toward the 50–52% pledge.
- Price the carbon externality — either through a federal clean electricity standard, a border adjustment, or a carbon fee — so that the ~$760B implicit subsidy shrinks rather than grows.
- Quadruple terrestrial protected area coverage to hit 30x30 via Tribal co-stewardship, OECM recognition for working lands under conservation easements, and expansion rather than shrinkage of Antiquities Act monuments.
- Modernize the Endangered Species Act implementation to restore strong critical habitat designations and reverse 2025 rule narrowing of "harm."
- Fully resource EPA, NOAA, USGS, and USDA's conservation arms, reversing the 2025 staffing and data purges that have weakened federal monitoring capacity.
- Treat methane as a tier-one pollutant: cap oil and gas methane at IRA-law levels, expand leak detection, and tackle enteric methane through research and standards.

## Data Confidence

**High.** US emissions, energy, protected area, and litigation data are all backed by multiple primary sources that agree closely (EPA inventory, EIA, Ember, Rhodium, Sabin Center, Protected Planet). The two weakest points are biodiversity (Red List and ESA coverage are strong but trend data lags) and indigenous land recognition (LandMark/RRI figures vary depending on whether reservation land is counted as "recognized"). The composite score's confidence is high, but the rapid policy volatility in 2025–2026 means the trajectory is moving faster than annual datasets can capture.

## Key Tension

The country with the greatest historical responsibility, the strongest science capacity, and the most advanced clean-tech investment program on paper is actively dismantling that program mid-execution, leaving states and courts to carry federal-scale obligations.
