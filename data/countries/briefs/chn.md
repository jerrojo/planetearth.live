## Overview

China is the central paradox of the planetary transition. It is simultaneously the world's largest annual greenhouse gas emitter (~15.1 GtCO2e, roughly 30% of the global total) and the world's largest builder of renewable energy — solar and wind combined overtook coal in installed capacity in September 2025. Whether the planet stays within 1.5°C is, in a very real sense, decided in Beijing. The signals are mixed. CO2 emissions have been flat-to-falling for 21 consecutive months, suggesting the peak may already be behind us, years ahead of the 2030 commitment. But coal still provides 55% of electricity, and new coal plants continue to receive approvals under a "coal as backup" doctrine. The 2035 NDC submitted in November 2025 was China's first-ever absolute emissions target — a historic shift — but at a 7–10% cut from peak, it falls far short of what a 1.5°C pathway requires.

Beyond climate, China is one of 17 megadiverse countries, home to pandas, snow leopards, Yangtze alligators, and an extraordinary concentration of endemic plant species. It chaired and hosted the Kunming round of the UN biodiversity negotiations that produced the 30×30 framework — yet its own protected areas stand at 15.61% terrestrial and 4.82% marine, well below the target it helped set. The afforestation story is more positive: forest cover has doubled from ~12% to ~24% since 1949 through the Grain-for-Green and Three-North Shelterbelt programs, one of the largest land-restoration efforts in human history, though heavily reliant on monoculture plantations that do less for biodiversity than native ecosystems would.

## Top 5 Gaps

**1. Emissions trajectory vs 1.5°C pathway (41% gap).** Under current policies, China is projected to emit ~13.7 GtCO2e in 2030. A 1.5°C-compatible pathway requires ~9.7 GtCO2e. Closing this gap requires the NDC to be tightened from "7–10% below peak" to roughly "30% below peak" and for coal retirements — not just additions of renewables — to begin in earnest.

**2. Coal share of electricity (55% vs <20% needed).** China added record renewable capacity in 2024 and 2025, but coal capacity also continued to grow. The fundamental bottleneck is provincial political economy: coal plants are assets of local state-owned enterprises, and their retirement involves employment, debt, and grid-balancing questions that have not been resolved. The IEA's Net Zero by 2050 pathway requires unabated coal to fall below 20% of electricity globally by 2030.

**3. Marine protected areas (4.82% vs 30%).** China's exclusive economic zone is vast, and its marine biodiversity — especially in the South China Sea and Yellow Sea — is under severe pressure from industrial fishing, aquaculture runoff, and coastal development. Progress on marine 30×30 is the weakest corner of China's biodiversity record.

**4. Terrestrial protected areas (15.61% vs 30%).** The 2021 launch of the National Park system (5 parks) is structurally significant but coverage remains thin. Scaling will require confronting land-tenure and livelihoods questions in rural western regions.

**5. Fossil fuel subsidies (~$50B explicit per IEA; ~$2.2T implicit per IMF).** G20 members pledged in 2009 to phase out inefficient fossil fuel subsidies. China's explicit subsidies remain among the world's largest, and IMF's implicit subsidy figure — which includes undertaxed externalities — puts China at the top of the global list.

## Recent Actions (last 12 months)

**Positive:**

- **March 2026:** Ecological and Environmental Code adopted — the first comprehensive codification of Chinese environmental law. Gives the 2060 net-zero pledge statutory force.
- **November 2025:** Submitted 2035 NDC at UNFCCC — first-ever absolute emissions target from China.
- **September 2025:** Solar + wind surpassed coal in installed capacity.
- **October 2025:** National carbon market expanded to cement, aluminum, and steel.
- **January 2026:** Yangtze fishing ban extended after early recovery signals in the finless porpoise population.

**Negative:**

- **July 2025:** 8.4 GW of new coal plants approved — contradicting the "coal as backup" framing.

## What a Green Pathway Would Look Like

If China closed its top gaps, it would:

- Tighten the 2035 NDC from a 7–10% cut to ~30% cut from peak, submitting an updated pledge before COP30.
- Cap and schedule coal retirements, not just additions of renewables, with a hard declining trajectory for coal-fired generation post-2027.
- Triple marine protected area coverage by 2030 to hit 30×30, with genuine no-take zones in critical fisheries.
- Extend the National Park system from 5 to 20+ parks and formally recognize community-conserved areas (OECMs) to close the terrestrial 30×30 gap.
- Phase out explicit fossil fuel subsidies, redirecting the ~$50B/yr into a just transition fund for coal-dependent regions.
- Open environmental reporting space — currently heavily constrained — so civil society and independent scientists can co-monitor progress.

## Data Confidence

**Medium.** Emissions, energy, and protected area data are high-confidence (multiple primary sources agree). Biodiversity and governance pillars rely on single-source indicators or contain known undercounts (defenders database shows zero, which almost certainly reflects reporting constraints rather than reality).

## Key Tension

The world's largest emitter is also the world's largest builder of renewables — whether China's energy transition reaches scale in time depends less on technology and more on whether coal is allowed to retire, not just be supplemented.
