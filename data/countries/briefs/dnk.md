# Denmark — Planet Lens Profile

**Composite Score: 59 / 100 — YELLOW**
**Last updated: 2026-04-20**

---

## Overview

Denmark is the country that most complicates the idea of a simple green ranking. On climate and governance it is a genuine frontrunner: emissions have fallen by roughly half since 1990, electricity is 84% renewable, wind alone now supplies more of the grid than any other source, and the 2020 Climate Act gave the 70%-by-2030 target the force of law — a more ambitious near-term goal than any other major economy has committed to. Yale's 2024 Environmental Performance Index placed Denmark in the global top five. The country co-founded the Beyond Oil and Gas Alliance in 2021, legislated an end-date for North Sea oil and gas, and in November 2024 signed the world's first carbon tax on livestock agriculture.

And yet Denmark's composite score sits in the yellow band, not the green. The reason is written into the landscape itself. Roughly 60% of the national territory is farmland — one of the highest shares in the world — and the farming model is extraordinarily intensive: about 12 million pigs on a human population of 5.9 million, nitrogen-heavy cereal and fodder systems, and dairy. Agriculture accounts for 27% of national greenhouse gas emissions, and agricultural nitrogen run-off is the proximate cause of chronic oxygen-starved "dead zones" in the Kattegat and the inner Danish fjords. Denmark's biodiversity intactness is among the lowest in Europe, and 41% of assessed species are threatened or in decline. The country leads the world on the electricity transition and trails on the land transition.

## Top 5 Gaps

**1. Agricultural emissions (55% cut needed).** Agriculture is the single largest obstacle to a credible 2030 pathway. At ~11 MtCO2e and 27% of the total, it is proportionally larger than in almost any other high-income country. The 2024 carbon tax is historic — no other country has priced livestock methane at the farm gate — but the headline rate of 300 DKK (~43 USD) per tCO2e only takes effect in 2035, and deductions cut the effective rate substantially in early years. A faster schedule, or an absolute cap on livestock numbers, is what the Climate Council says is needed.

**2. 2030 target gap (18%).** Even the "almost sufficient" CAT rating comes with a caveat: under current policies, Denmark reaches roughly 63% below 1990, not 70%. Transport electrification is slower than planned, and building-sector heat-pump rollout has lagged supply-chain expectations. Closing the final gap will require faster EV adoption, a carbon price floor on heating, and non-trivial agricultural reductions.

**3. Strict protection of nature (2.3% vs 10%).** Denmark's protected-area coverage looks respectable at 18.6% on paper, but most of that area is still farmed, hunted, or commercially forested. The EU Biodiversity Strategy asks for 10% of EU land under strict protection by 2030. Denmark is nowhere near that. The 15-park Nature National Park programme launched in 2024 is the right shape of answer but covers only ~250 km² so far.

**4. Coastal nitrogen pollution (32% over target).** Roughly 56,000 tonnes of nitrogen leach into Danish marine waters each year, primarily from farm fertilizer. Water Framework Directive "good status" requires roughly 38,000 tonnes. Algal blooms and oxygen depletion killed large areas of bottom life in the Limfjord and Kattegat through 2023–2024. Agricultural reform is the lever — and the same lever as the climate problem.

**5. Biodiversity trajectory (41% of species in decline).** Denmark's landscape has been so thoroughly reshaped by intensive agriculture that there is little connected habitat left. Farmland bird populations have collapsed by roughly 40% since 1990. Turning this around requires restoring wetlands, hedgerows, and uncultivated margins at a scale well beyond current plans.

## Recent Actions (last 12 months)

**Positive:**

- **November 2024:** Green Tripartite Agreement — world's first carbon tax on livestock agriculture, paired with 250,000 hectares of reforestation and wetland restoration.
- **February 2026:** BOGA ministerial reaffirmed 2050 end-date for Danish North Sea production and pressed non-signatories to join.
- **March 2026:** 15th Nature National Park designated; rewilded land area at ~250 km².

**Neutral / Mixed:**

- **May 2025:** Climate Council's 2025 Status Report — on track but with a 5 MtCO2e gap; transport and agriculture flagged as under-delivering.

**Negative:**

- **September 2025:** Copenhagen officially conceded it missed its flagship 2025 carbon-neutral target — a high-profile failure driven by biomass CCS non-delivery.
- **November 2025:** Ørsted, Denmark's offshore-wind national champion, scaled back its US pipeline and cut 25% of its workforce — signal of wider stress in the offshore wind sector.

## What a Green Pathway Would Look Like

If Denmark closed its top gaps, it would:

- Accelerate the livestock carbon tax schedule, bringing the full rate forward to 2030 and removing the large early-year deductions.
- Set a statutory ceiling on national pig and dairy herd size, coupled with just-transition support for farmers to move into rewilded land, agroforestry, and plant-protein production.
- Expand strictly protected land from 2.3% to 10% by 2030, with clear no-commercial-use status, and continue rewilding existing national parks.
- Deliver a nitrogen action plan that meets Water Framework Directive targets by 2027, primarily through reduced fertilizer use and wetland buffer restoration.
- Reach 100% fossil-free electricity by 2030 (currently 94%) and complete the coal phase-out at Fynsværket and the remaining plants.
- Maintain global leadership on BOGA — push G7 and major producers to adopt supply-side fossil phase-out commitments.

## Data Confidence

**High.** Denmark is one of the best-instrumented countries in the world for environmental data. Emissions, energy mix, pollution, and protected areas are all triangulated across EEA, Ember, Climate Council, and Protected Planet. Biodiversity is the one pillar where medium confidence is warranted — the national Red List is strong but agricultural-soil biodiversity remains under-monitored.

## Key Tension

Denmark leads the world on clean electricity and climate governance, but its pork-and-dairy agricultural model — 60% of the land, a third of emissions, and the engine of coastal dead zones — is the reason the same country cannot call itself a green one.
