**Composite Score: 35 / 100 — RED**
**Last updated: 2026-04-20**

## Overview

Vietnam is the country where the global clean-energy transition's most ambitious diplomacy meets its most uncomfortable political contradictions. In December 2022 it signed the second Just Energy Transition Partnership (JETP), a $15.5 billion climate-finance package from the G7, EU, Denmark, and Norway — the flagship test of whether rich-country capital can pull a fast-growing coal-reliant economy onto a 1.5°C-compatible path. In the same window, the very civil-society leaders whose research and advocacy had made the JETP case — Nguy Thi Khanh (Goldman Environmental Prize 2018), Hong Hoang, Dang Dinh Bach, Mai Phan Loi, and Bach Hung Duong — were arrested on tax and administrative charges widely viewed by international observers as reprisal. The transition is now being negotiated without its independent monitors.

On the ground, the signals are mixed and mostly worrying. Vietnam's rooftop-solar boom of 2018–2020 was extraordinary — the country added more solar capacity in two years than it had coal in its entire history — but ended abruptly with grid-integration failures, curtailment of up to 40% in some southern provinces, and a freeze on new feed-in tariffs. Coal still generated 44% of electricity in 2025, and two new LNG power plants approved in August 2025 will lock in fossil-gas infrastructure into the 2050s. Power Development Plan 8 (2023, revised 2024) caps coal capacity at ~30 GW — but the existing pipeline already hits that ceiling.

Vietnam is not on the official 17-country megadiverse list, but it is nonetheless extraordinarily biodiverse: the saola ("Asian unicorn," rediscovered in 1992 and possibly fewer than 100 individuals remaining), Cao Vit gibbon, Delacour's langur, Indochinese tiger (likely functionally extinct in Vietnam's wild since ~2014), and the Javan rhino (declared extinct in Vietnam in 2011). The country sits at the centre of Southeast Asia's illegal wildlife trade and loses primary forest in the Central Highlands to coffee, rubber, and acacia plantations even as headline forest cover rises to ~42%.

## Top 5 Gaps

**1. Emissions trajectory vs 2030 NDC (39% overshoot).** Current policies put Vietnam at ~560 MtCO2e in 2030 against an unconditional cap of 403.7 MtCO2e. Closing the gap requires not more pledges but coal retirements, meaningful gas-plant cancellation, and unfreezing the stranded rooftop-solar fleet through long-delayed Direct Power Purchase Agreement (DPPA) rules.

**2. Coal share of electricity (44% vs under 10% needed).** PDP8's cap is real but non-binding at the plant level, and provincial authorities continue to approve gas — effectively substituting one fossil dependency for another. Vietnamese coal plants are young (average age ~12 years), so early retirement involves stranded-asset costs that JETP finance is supposed to address but has yet to deploy at scale.

**3. Marine protected areas (0.5% vs 30%).** Vietnam has one of the lowest marine protection rates in ASEAN. Coastal biodiversity in the Gulf of Tonkin and around Ha Long Bay is under extreme pressure from industrial aquaculture, plastic waste, and tourism, and enforcement in the existing MPAs is chronically underfunded.

**4. Terrestrial protected areas (8.2% vs 30%).** The national-park system is well-established on paper but management effectiveness is weak. Primary-forest loss in the Central Highlands — a biodiversity hotspot — continues despite headline forest-cover gains, because plantation acacia and eucalyptus inflate the denominator.

**5. Civic space for environmental defenders.** JETP's credibility depends on independent monitoring, but Vietnam has jailed the country's most respected climate-transition advocates. Until at minimum Dang Dinh Bach and the remaining detained advocates are released, the gap between JETP's theory of change and its practice is structural rather than technical.

## Recent Actions (last 12 months)

**Positive:**

- **September 2025:** Revised PDP8 implementation plan accelerated offshore wind (6 GW by 2030) and piloted first LNG-to-renewable conversions.
- **December 2025:** Expanded 1-million-hectare low-emission rice program in the Mekong Delta with World Bank results-based finance.

**Neutral:**

- **November 2025:** JETP Resource Mobilisation Plan updated — ~$8.1B of $15.5B now allocated, but disbursement remains slow and loan-heavy.

**Negative:**

- **August 2025:** Approved Nhon Trach 3 and 4 LNG power plants (1.5 GW), locking in fossil-gas infrastructure.
- **June 2025:** Tightened media and NGO registration rules, further constraining independent environmental reporting.
- **February 2026:** Climate advocate Dang Dinh Bach completed his fourth year of imprisonment on tax charges widely viewed as reprisal.

## What a Green Pathway Would Look Like

If Vietnam closed its top gaps, it would:

- Tighten the 2030 NDC to match its JETP-implied trajectory (peak before 2030 at ~450 Mt), with binding annual coal-generation ceilings rather than capacity caps.
- Cancel unbuilt LNG plants, retire the oldest coal units first, and finalize the long-delayed DPPA rules to unfreeze rooftop-solar investment.
- Triple terrestrial protected area coverage and grow marine protection twenty-fold by 2030 — especially around the Mekong Delta, Ha Long Bay, and Con Dao — with genuine no-take enforcement and community co-management.
- Scale the low-emission rice program from 1 Mha to the full ~4 Mha of Mekong paddy, and pair Central Highlands coffee/pepper growth with deforestation-free sourcing under EUDR.
- Release Dang Dinh Bach and remaining detained environmental advocates, formally legalise climate NGO registration, and bring Vietnamese civil society into the JETP Political Declaration's co-monitoring structure.
- Phase out the ~$4.2B/yr in explicit fossil subsidies and redirect them into a just-transition fund for coal regions like Quang Ninh.

## Data Confidence

**Medium.** Emissions, energy, forest-cover, and protected-area data are high-confidence (OWID, Ember, GFW, and Protected Planet converge). Biodiversity data is weaker — Vietnam's species monitoring outside flagship sites is patchy — and the intersection signals understate reality: the defenders-killed figure is 0 because Vietnam's repression takes the form of imprisonment and judicial harassment rather than lethal violence, which global databases capture inconsistently.

## Key Tension

Vietnam signed the world's second-largest JETP deal ($15.5B) while simultaneously jailing the civil-society leaders who built the case for it — making the transition a top-down bargain without the independent monitoring that would make it credible.
