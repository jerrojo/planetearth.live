## Overview

South Korea occupies an unusual position in the climate accounting. It is a wealthy, technologically sophisticated democracy with per-capita emissions of roughly 12.4 tCO2e — higher than Germany, France, or Japan — and an industrial base (steel, petrochemicals, semiconductor fabs, shipbuilding) whose energy intensity anchors the national footprint. Absolute emissions have been drifting down since a 2018 peak, but the decline owes more to post-pandemic industrial cycles and a partial nuclear recovery than to a structural renewables build-out. Solar and wind together produced roughly a tenth of electricity in 2024, one of the lowest shares in the OECD. Coal still supplies around 30% of the grid, gas another 28%, and the Yoon and current administrations have pivoted hard back toward nuclear — eleven new large reactors plus small modular reactors (SMRs) are in the pipeline under the 11th Basic Electricity Plan.

Against that, the governance signals are genuinely strong. The 2021 Framework Act on Carbon Neutrality placed the 2050 net-zero target in law. In August 2024, the Constitutional Court became the first in Asia to rule a national climate statute partly unconstitutional, finding that the absence of quantified reduction targets for 2031–2049 violated the rights of future generations. The ruling forces Seoul to legislate a credible post-2030 trajectory before the end of February 2026, and a revised NDC is being prepared for pre-COP31 submission. One storyline to watch: whether that revision actually tightens the trajectory or merely formalises the existing gap.

The country's forestry legacy is striking. After the Korean War, hillsides across the peninsula had been stripped almost bare; a half-century of state-driven planting rebuilt forest cover from roughly a third of the land to nearly two-thirds, one of the great reforestation successes of the 20th century. That forest is now aging, saturating as a carbon sink, and increasingly vulnerable to climate-amplified wildfires — the March 2025 Uiseong–Andong fires were the largest in Korean history, burning over 48,000 hectares.

## Top 5 Gaps

**1. Emissions trajectory vs 1.5°C pathway (67% gap).** Current policies put 2030 emissions at roughly 600 MtCO2e, while a Paris-aligned pathway for a country at South Korea's wealth and capability level is closer to 360 MtCO2e. Closing this requires both a faster industrial decarbonisation package (green steel, green hydrogen for petrochemicals) and a coal retirement timetable that does not exist today.

**2. Renewables share of electricity (10% vs >60% needed).** Permitting timelines for onshore wind average six to eight years, community opposition is structured into the process, and the grid's historical design around centralised fossil plants and nuclear has left chronic curtailment in Jeju and the southwest. Offshore wind is the one bright spot, with corporate PPAs from Korea Zinc, POSCO, and SK unlocking a meaningful demand signal.

**3. Coal share of electricity (30% and no phase-out date).** The OECD phase-out benchmark for high-income members is 2030. South Korea has committed to stop public finance for overseas coal (delivered in 2021) but not to retire domestic coal plants, many of which are relatively young KEPCO assets with 15–25 years of remaining book life.

**4. Marine protected areas (1.8% vs 30%).** The gap here is among the widest of any OECD economy. Coastal fisheries lobbying, aquaculture expansion, and weak jurisdictional coordination across the Yellow, East, and South Seas have stalled progress, even as tidal flats continue to be lost to reclamation.

**5. Fossil fuel subsidies (~$14B explicit).** Tax exemptions on industrial LPG, bunker fuels, and agricultural diesel, combined with household electricity cross-subsidies, place South Korea among the top OECD subsidisers per GDP. The redirection of those flows into a just-transition fund for coal regions like Chungnam has been proposed repeatedly but never enacted.

## Recent Actions (last 12 months)

**Positive:**

- **August 2024:** Constitutional Court ruling on the Carbon Neutrality Act — the most consequential climate judgment in Asia to date.
- **November 2025:** Wave of corporate offshore-wind PPAs (Korea Zinc, POSCO, SK) collectively supporting ~4 GW of build-out.
- **February 2026:** Government signals a tightened 2035 NDC for pre-COP31 submission, in compliance with the Constitutional Court ruling.

**Negative:**

- **February 2025:** 11th Basic Electricity Plan finalised — doubles down on nuclear and SMRs, leaves coal in the generation mix past 2035, and modestly lifts renewables ambition.
- **March 2025:** Uiseong–Andong wildfires, largest in national history — a climate-adaptation shortfall as much as a disaster.
- **December 2025:** KEPCO approves three new LNG terminals and 4.8 GW of gas capacity, creating multi-decade fossil lock-in.

## What a Green Pathway Would Look Like

If South Korea closed its top gaps, it would:

- Legislate a quantified 2035 NDC of roughly -60 to -65% from 2018, consistent with the Constitutional Court mandate and a 1.5°C pathway.
- Publish a binding coal-retirement schedule with the last unabated plant offline by 2030, paired with a just-transition package for Chungnam and Gyeongnam.
- Halve permitting timelines for onshore wind and solar through a one-stop national authority, unlocking the ~40 GW of stalled pipeline.
- Triple offshore wind targets and ring-fence transmission corridors in the 12th Basic Plan.
- Expand marine protected areas to 10% by 2028 on a path to 30% by 2030, starting with enforceable no-take zones in the West Sea.
- Reform industrial energy tax exemptions and redeploy the recovered revenue to hydrogen-based steel and petrochemical retooling.

## Data Confidence

**High.** Emissions, energy, and protected area data are well-corroborated across Ember, OWID, IEA, and Protected Planet. Forestry statistics from the Korea Forest Service are detailed and consistent. Biodiversity and agricultural pillars lean more heavily on national single-source indicators and are rated medium. The Global Witness figure of zero defender killings reflects a low-risk civic environment rather than an undercount.

## Key Tension

A high-income democracy with the institutional capacity, industrial base, and supreme court mandate to lead Asia on climate is instead extending coal, doubling down on LNG, and relying on a nuclear revival to paper over a renewables deployment that remains among the OECD's slowest.
